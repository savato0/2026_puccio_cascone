\begin{abstract}
We collected reply threads from Bluesky on four topics (politics, sports, technology and entertainment) and built a network where two users are connected if one replied to the other. Every reply has a sentiment score computed with a RoBERTa model trained on tweets, and for the users who received replies we also downloaded their recent replies on the whole platform. The network has 13,277 nodes and 17,522 undirected edges. Compared with Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi and Barab\'asi-Albert graphs of the same size, it has a heavy-tailed degree distribution, short distances and a higher clustering. Leiden finds 61 communities that mostly follow the topics. In the open question we asked whether negative or polarised users are more central: on 2,927 users the answer is no, while central accounts attract more extreme replies.

\section{Introduction}
Bluesky is a micro-blogging platform built on the decentralised AT Protocol. Differently from other platforms, its data are public and can be collected through an open API, which makes it a good source for network studies \cite{failla2024bluesky}.

The goal of our project is to study how users reply to each other and whether the tone of these replies is related to the structure of the network. The open question asks whether there is a correlation between sentiment (in particular negativity or anger) and the structural importance of a node. In this report we describe the data collection (Section~\ref{sec:data}), the analysis of the network compared with random graphs (Section~\ref{sec:net}), the community detection task (Section~\ref{sec:cd}) and the open question (Section~\ref{sec:oq}).

\section{Data Collection}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Selected Data Sources}
We used the public API of Bluesky. A node is a Bluesky account and a directed edge $(u,v)$ means that $u$ replied at least once to a post written by $v$. For every edge we saved the texts of all the replies from $u$ to $v$ (\texttt{comments\_list}), their number (\texttt{weight}) and their mean sentiment (\texttt{sentiment\_roberta}). We also saved a table with one row per reply (author, target, text, date, language, thread, topic), which is the primary data of the open question. For the users we saved the number of followers, whether they wrote a seed post, the main topic of their threads and, for a subset described below, their recent replies on the whole platform.

\subsection{Crawling Methodology and Assumptions}
The code is in the folder \texttt{crawl\_v2}. The crawl was run on 3 October 2026 in four steps.

\textbf{Seeds.} We searched eight keywords, two per topic: \textit{trump} and \textit{election} (politics), \textit{nfl} and \textit{football} (sports), \textit{ai} and \textit{iphone} (technology), \textit{movie} and \textit{music} (entertainment). We kept English posts written between 14 and 28 September 2026, which are not replies and have at least 5 replies, sorted by relevance. The search is the only step that requires a login; we used the first 30 posts per keyword, 240 seeds in total.

\textbf{Threads.} For each seed we downloaded the whole thread (\texttt{getPostThread}, maximum depth). Every reply becomes an edge from its author to the author of the post it answers. As in the first version of our crawler, self-replies and replies shorter than 5 characters do not create edges. The API did not return 23\% of the declared replies: in most cases one reply per post, which was deleted or hidden, plus one very large thread (more than 3,000 replies) that the API truncates.

\textbf{Timelines.} For the 3,163 users who received at least one reply, and for 1,000 other users chosen at random, we downloaded their last replies on Bluesky (\texttt{getAuthorFeed}, about 50 replies per user). These texts are used only to measure the tone of each user in the open question and never become edges, so the centrality of a user does not depend on how we measure its sentiment.

\textbf{Profiles.} For all the 13,945 authors we downloaded the number of followers and the creation date of the account.

\textbf{Limitations of the sampling.} The sample is not random. The seeds are popular posts, so their authors become hubs with hundreds of neighbours; in the open question we treat the 192 seed authors separately. The threads cover two weeks, and politics is the largest topic (41\% of the nodes, against 16--22\% for the other three).

\subsection{Sentiment Enrichment}\label{sec:sentiment}
We computed the sentiment of every reply with \texttt{cardiffnlp/}\allowbreak\texttt{twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest}, a RoBERTa model trained on tweets \cite{loureiro2022timelms}. For each reply the model returns the probabilities of the classes negative, neutral and positive; we use $s = p_{pos} - p_{neg} \in [-1,+1]$. The sentiment of an edge is the mean of its replies. We also scored every reply with an emotion classifier of the same family (\texttt{twitter-roberta-base-emotion}: anger, joy, optimism, sadness). The labels in the configuration file of this model on the hub are in the wrong order, so we fixed them and checked them on four control sentences.

\subsection{Data Understanding}
The network contains 19,761 replies (notebook \texttt{dataUnderstanding.ipynb}). The median length is 62 characters; 13.2\% of the replies are shorter than 20 characters and 59.6\% have at least 50. Most pairs of users (95.2\%) exchanged a single reply, and the maximum weight is 11. 97\% of the replies are declared in English.

The tone is negative on average but less than in political threads only: the mean edge sentiment is $-0.24$ and the median $-0.37$; 44\% of the edges are below $-0.5$ and 16\% above $+0.5$. The share of negative replies is 55\% in politics and sports, 43\% in technology and 35\% in entertainment. The score measures the tone of the text and not the relation between the two users: two users who agree while criticising someone both get a negative score.

\subsection{Final Dataset}
The directed network has 13,277 nodes and 18,586 edges, without self-loops. For the analysis we use the undirected, unweighted and simple version: the 1,064 pairs of users who replied to each other become a single edge, so the undirected graph has 17,522 edges. When two directed edges are merged, their sentiment is averaged with the number of replies as weight.

\section{Network Characterization}\label{sec:net}
We compared our network with two random graphs with the same number of nodes $N=13{,}277$: an Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi (ER) graph \cite{erdos1959random} with $p = 2M/(N(N-1)) \approx 1.99 \cdot 10^{-4}$, so that the expected number of edges is ours, and a Barab\'asi-Albert (BA) graph \cite{barabasi1999emergence}. For BA, $m$ must be an integer and $M/N \approx 1.32$, so we used $m=1$: the result is a tree with 24\% fewer edges than our graph and no triangles. The basic statistics are in Table~\ref{tab:basic}.

\subsection{Degree Distribution}
The average degree is the same for Bluesky and ER, but the standard deviation and the maximum are very different: in ER no node has more than 11 neighbours, while in our network the most connected account has 906. Figure~\ref{fig:degdist} shows the degree distribution and Figure~\ref{fig:ccdf} its complementary cumulative distribution (CCDF).

\end{figure*}

\begin{figure}[h]

  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{degree_ccdf.png}

We fitted a power law $P(k) \propto k^{-\alpha}$ with the \texttt{powerlaw} library \cite{alstott2014powerlaw}, which uses maximum likelihood \cite{clauset2009powerlaw}. The library chooses $x_{min}=1$, so the fit uses all the nodes, and gives $\alpha \approx 2.36$. With the likelihood ratio test ($R>0$ favours the power law), the power law is better than the exponential ($R=9.7$, $p \approx 2\cdot10^{-22}$) and the lognormal ($R=4.6$, $p \approx 3\cdot10^{-6}$), while it cannot be distinguished from a truncated power law ($R=0.005$, $p=0.99$). ER has the Poisson shape expected for a random graph, while BA has the power-law tail of preferential attachment.

\subsection{Connected Components}
Our network has 41 connected components. The giant component (GCC) contains 12,916 nodes (97.3\%), the second one 84. The ER graph with the same density is much more fragmented: 1,060 components, a GCC with 90.9\% of the nodes and 951 isolated nodes. BA is connected by construction. Paths and centralities are computed on the GCC of each graph.

\subsection{Path Analysis}
We computed the exact average shortest path and diameter with a BFS from every node:
\begin{itemize}
\item Bluesky: average distance 5.02, diameter 16;
\item ER: average distance 9.61, diameter 23;
\item BA: average distance 9.05, diameter 23.
\end{itemize}
With the same density as ER, our network has distances that are about half, because most paths go through a few hubs. Together with the clustering below, this is the typical behaviour of a small-world network. The hubs are partly created by the sampling, since they are the authors of the seed posts.

\subsection{Clustering and Density}
The density is very low ($1.99 \cdot 10^{-4}$) and the same as ER. The clustering coefficients are in Table~\ref{tab:clust}.

\begin{table}[h]
  \caption{Average clustering coefficient and transitivity.}
  \label{tab:clust}
  \begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
     & Bluesky & ER & BA \\
    \midrule
    avg. clustering & 0.0441 & 0.0001 & 0 \\
    transitivity & 0.0012 & 0.0001 & 0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The average clustering is hundreds of times higher than in ER, while the transitivity is higher but still very small. The two measures differ because the average clustering gives the same weight to every node, while the transitivity counts connected triples. Only 1,197 nodes are in at least one triangle, and 77\% of them have degree 5 or less. On the other hand, 94\% of the connected triples are centred on the 50 nodes with the highest degree, whose neighbours almost never reply to each other. Triadic closure exists, but it is weak and limited to small groups of users.

\subsection{Centrality Analysis}
On the GCC we computed five centrality measures.

\textbf{Degree.} The top ten accounts are \texttt{ronfilipkowski} (906), \texttt{atrupar.com} (867), \texttt{sundaedivine.lol} (482), \texttt{marcelias} (373), \texttt{therickydavila} (351), \texttt{onecaliberal} (320), \texttt{thetnholler} (315), \texttt{meidastouch.com} (308), \texttt{muellershewrote.com} (262) and \texttt{thegodshow.com} (214). Most of them are political commentators and news accounts: political threads are larger than the others.

\textbf{Betweenness} (estimated from 500 random sources) and \textbf{closeness} are highest for \texttt{atrupar.com} (0.19 and 0.31), followed by \texttt{ronfilipkowski} (0.18 and 0.31).

\textbf{Eigenvector centrality and PageRank.} Eigenvector centrality is concentrated on the two biggest hubs (0.57 and 0.41, all the other nodes below 0.05), because the score of a node depends on its neighbours. PageRank, thanks to the random jumps, is more spread.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{centrality_correlation.png}
  \caption{Spearman correlation between the five centrality measures on the GCC.}
  \label{fig:cent}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Correlation.} Degree, betweenness and PageRank are strongly correlated (0.77--0.92, Figure~\ref{fig:cent}); closeness and eigenvector centrality are correlated with each other (0.96) but weakly with the others (0.21--0.30), and PageRank is not correlated with them ($-0.03$). Closeness and eigenvector are high for every node close to the biggest hubs, also with degree 1: the 545 degree-1 neighbours of \texttt{ronfilipkowski} have a higher median closeness (0.23 vs 0.20) and eigenvector centrality (0.018 vs 0.0002) than the other 8,175 degree-1 nodes, but the same PageRank.

\subsection{Comparison with ER and BA}
Our network is sparse, has a giant component with almost all the nodes, a heavy-tailed degree distribution, short distances and more clustering than random graphs. ER reproduces the density but not the hubs, the distances and the clustering; BA has hubs but, with $m=1$, fewer edges, longer distances and no triangles.

\section{Task 1: Community Detection}\label{sec:cd}
Our group has two members, so we chose one task of Part 3. We chose community detection because the GCC contains almost all the nodes and because the topic of the threads and the sentiment of the replies can be used to interpret the communities. All the algorithms run on the unweighted GCC ($N=12{,}916$, $M=17{,}196$).

\subsection{Algorithms}
We used four algorithms from \texttt{cdlib} \cite{rossetti2019cdlib}: \textbf{Louvain} \cite{blondel2008louvain}, which greedily optimises modularity; \textbf{Leiden} \cite{traag2019leiden}, an improvement of Louvain that guarantees connected communities (seed 42); \textbf{Infomap} \cite{rosvall2008infomap}, which finds the partition that best compresses a random walk (option \texttt{-{}-two-level}, for a flat partition); and \textbf{Label Propagation} \cite{raghavan2007lpa}, in the semi-synchronous version \cite{cordasco2010semisync}, which is deterministic.

\subsection{Internal Evaluation}
For each partition we computed the number and size of the communities, the Newman-Girvan modularity $Q$, the average conductance (fraction of a community's edges that go outside; lower is better) and the internal edge density (Table~\ref{tab:cd}).

Louvain and Leiden find about 60 communities of a few hundred nodes, with modularity 0.79 and low conductance: the network has a clear community structure. Infomap and Label Propagation find hundreds of small communities (median size 6 and 2); their higher internal density only reflects their small size, and their lower modularity is not a fair comparison, since they do not optimise it.

\subsection{Comparison between Algorithms}
We compared the partitions with the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) and the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), which is corrected for chance (Figure~\ref{fig:agree}). Louvain and Leiden are similar (NMI 0.86, ARI 0.77), and so are Infomap and Label Propagation (NMI 0.84, ARI 0.61). Between the two groups the NMI is still high (0.72--0.83) but the ARI is lower (0.46--0.57). Since NMI tends to be high when one partition has many small communities, we give more weight to ARI: Infomap and Label Propagation seem to split the larger communities of Louvain and Leiden.

They do: 81\% of the edges connect two users whose threads have the same topic, and on average 76\% of the nodes of a community share the same topic (weighted by size). The tone is negative in 52 of the 61 communities (mean $-0.20$, median $-0.27$), but it depends on the topic: the mean sentiment of the communities is $-0.33$ for sports, $-0.27$ for politics, $-0.15$ for entertainment and $-0.03$ for technology. A political community can also be positive: the one around \texttt{marcelias} has a mean of $+0.34$, probably because the replies support the author of the thread. Larger communities tend to be slightly more negative (Spearman $\rho=-0.24$, $p=0.06$). As for the first dataset, a negative tone inside a community does not mean that its members are hostile to each other.

\section{Task 2: Open Question}\label{sec:oq}
The approved question is: \textit{is there a correlation between sentiment (in particular negativity or anger) and the structural importance of a node?} In particular, we wanted to check whether users with a more polarised sentiment have a higher in-degree, betweenness and PageRank than moderate users. The code is in \texttt{open\_question.ipynb}.

\subsection{Methodology}
\textbf{Sentiment of a user.} An edge $u \to v$ carries the tone written by $u$, while in-degree and PageRank measure how much $u$ receives. We measure the tone of a user on its \emph{timeline replies}, i.e. its last replies on all of Bluesky, excluding those that are edges of our network. We use three measures: the share of negative replies (\emph{negativity}), the mean of $s$, and the mean of $|s|$, which we call \emph{polarisation} because it is high both for very negative and very positive replies. A user is \emph{polarised} or \emph{moderate} if it is in the top or bottom tercile of polarisation.

\textbf{Population.} Users with at least 10 English timeline replies and at least one reply received in the network: 2,927 users, including 95\% of the 200 most replied-to accounts (sensitivity: at least 5 and at least 20 replies).

\textbf{Centrality.} In-degree and PageRank on the directed weighted graph, betweenness from Section~\ref{sec:net}, and the number of followers as a measure of popularity outside our sample.

\textbf{Tests.} Before looking at the results we declared one primary test: negativity vs.\ in-degree. For every test we report the Spearman correlation with a 95\% bootstrap interval and a permutation test, in which the scores are shuffled among all the timeline replies while the network and the number of replies of each user stay fixed (1,000 times). We also compute the correlation inside the Leiden communities, and a linear regression of $\log(1+\text{in-degree})$ on the sentiment controlling for activity (replies written in the network), followers, seed authorship and topic. The 28 secondary tests are corrected with the Holm method.

\textbf{Written tone and centrality.} The primary test is significant but the correlation is negligible ($\rho=-0.05$), and its sign comes from the authors of the seed posts. They are central by construction of the crawl (median in-degree 32) and write less negatively than the other users (33\% of negative replies against 47\%). Without them the correlation becomes slightly positive ($+0.05$), and polarisation is no longer related to in-degree ($\rho=-0.01$, $p=0.70$). Polarised and moderate users have the same median in-degree (1). In the regression with all the controls, one standard deviation of negativity changes $\log(1+\text{in-degree})$ by $+0.016$ (CI $[-0.001, +0.032]$), i.e.\ nothing; polarisation has a very small negative effect ($-0.022$). In comparison, writing a seed post multiplies the expected in-degree by about 15, and the activity of a user is also a strong predictor. Betweenness is not related to any measure of tone.

\textbf{PageRank and followers.} The largest correlations are with PageRank (negativity $-0.15$): users who write in a more positive tone are replied to by users who are themselves replied to, i.e.\ they take part in longer conversations. Users with more followers write in a more positive and more polarised tone ($\rho=+0.12$). These are secondary results and remain significant after the Holm correction.

\textbf{Received sentiment.} Central accounts attract replies that are more extreme in both directions: among the 336 users with at least 5 replies received, polarisation of the received replies grows with in-degree ($\rho=+0.13$, CI $[+0.03, +0.24]$, permutation $p=0.015$), while their negativity does not ($\rho=0.00$). The same result was found on the first dataset ($+0.17$).

\textbf{Anger.} Even with four topics, 79--82\% of the negative replies are classified as anger and, at user level, the shares of angry and negative replies correlate at 0.88. The results with anger are the same as with negativity, so we cannot separate the two.

\textbf{Communities.} Replies inside communities are slightly less negative than replies between communities ($-0.24$ vs $-0.30$, Cohen's $d=0.12$). The assortativity of the users' timeline negativity, which does not use the edges themselves, is only $r=0.07$.

\subsection{Answer to the Question}
With the tone of almost 3,000 users measured independently of the network, we find no meaningful correlation between how negative or polarised a user writes and its structural importance. The small correlation in the raw data is produced by the sampling, through the authors of the seed posts. The structural importance of a user depends on whether it started a thread and on how much it replies, not on its tone. What changes with centrality is the tone a user \emph{receives}: central accounts attract more extreme replies. On the first dataset we had found that users who write more negatively receive fewer replies ($\rho=-0.28$ on 102 users); with the new data this result does not hold, and the tone of a user measured inside the sampled threads agrees only weakly with the tone of its timeline ($\rho=0.26$).

\section{Discussion}
We built a reply network of 13,277 Bluesky users from threads on four topics. Compared with random graphs of the same size, it has a heavy-tailed degree distribution ($\alpha \approx 2.36$), distances that are about half of ER, a higher clustering and a clear community structure ($Q \approx 0.79$), with communities that largely follow the topics of the threads. In the open question we found that the tone a user writes is not related to its centrality, while central accounts receive more extreme replies.

The main limitation is still the sampling: the hubs are the authors of popular seed posts, so their centrality is partly produced by the crawl, and in-degree is 1 for most of the other users, which leaves little variation to explain. Moreover, the API did not return 23\% of the declared replies, the threads cover two weeks, politics is over-represented, and the sentiment and emotion models were trained on Twitter data and were not validated by hand on our replies. Compared with our first collection, saving one row per reply, the timelines of the users and their followers is what made the open question answerable.
